## Connection request acceptance rates, benchmarked

Before we get into templates, let’s set a real-world baseline.

[Across 96,051 campaigns](https://www.heyreach.io/benchmark-report), the typical LinkedIn outreach funnel looks like this:

* **Acceptance rate:** 21%. One in five requests gets accepted.
* **Reply rate:** 22%. Just over a fifth of the conversations you start get a reply.
* **Reply-to-acceptance conversion:** 18%. Of everyone who accepts, fewer than one in five ever writes back.

The biggest drop in the funnel happens after acceptance, not before. In fact, 10.7% of campaigns with accepted connections got zero replies. Getting someone to accept your request is only half the job. [What you say next](https://www.heyreach.io/blog/how-to-follow-up-on-linkedin) is where most campaigns fall apart.

This is why the quality of connection messages matters as much as the quality of follow-up messages. The two are inseparable.

## **How to write a LinkedIn connection request message: 4 strategies that work**

I’ve tested a range of approaches within my own professional network and at varying degrees of connection. What I keep coming back to is this: the best connection messages don’t feel like messages. They feel like the natural start of a conversation.

Here are the four strategies I use most:

### **1. Empty connection request**

This is my personal favorite. Many people assume it’s a strategy reserved for influencers with large followings, but that’s not been my experience at all.

When your LinkedIn profile is [polished and speaks for itself](https://www.heyreach.io/blog/profiles-history-affects-acceptance-rate), sending a connection request without a note can work surprisingly well. It’s like a nod across the room - non-intrusive, but deliberate.

**Why it works:**

* It’s efficient. No risk of saying something off-putting before the relationship even starts.
* It feels more organic. Most real-life connections don’t begin with a formal pitch.
* Strong profiles carry the message on their own.

### **2. Common ground**

This approach means finding a shared angle, a mutual connection, a common interest, an industry overlap, and leading with it. It’s one of the most reliable ways to make a message feel relevant rather than random. It’s a core part of any serious [lead generation strategy](https://www.heyreach.io/blog/linkedin-lead-generation-strategy) on LinkedIn.

**Why it works:**

* Shared context creates immediate relevance.
* It reads as the start of a conversation, not a cold pitch.

### **3. Compliments**

A genuine, specific compliment on a post, article, career move, or milestone sets a positive tone before you’ve asked for anything. Generic praise often reads as automated and does more harm than good.

A well-placed compliment strengthens an already solid connection message. It rarely saves a weak one.

**Why it works:**

* Makes the recipient more open to engaging.
* Creates a natural thread to pull on in follow-up.

### **4. Short and sweet**

When in doubt, be brief. A tight two-sentence message that communicates clearly what you want and why you’re reaching out will almost always outperform a long, overwrought one.

LinkedIn caps the note on a connection request at 200 characters on a free account, 300 if you're on [Premium, Sales Navigator or Recruiter](https://www.heyreach.io/blog/linkedin-account-types). Either way, that's room for two sentences, maybe three. Say [the one thing](https://www.heyreach.io/blog/linkedin-account-types) that earns a reply and stop.

**Why it works:**

* Easier to read. Easier to say yes to.
* Forces you to be precise about what you actually want.

No single approach wins in every situation. The skill is knowing which one to reach for based on context, the person, and the goal.

## **11 LinkedIn connection message templates**

These are the templates I’ve used to grow my network from essentially zero to 6k. Each one is designed for a specific scenario and intent. Each demonstrates different [types of sentences with examples](https://essaypro.com/blog/types-of-sentences) to achieve various connection goals, from building rapport to proposing collaborations. Pay attention to how the phrasing shifts to suit the specific intent of the message.

### **Template 1: connecting on common ground**

> *Hi [Name],
>
> I see we're both into [Common Interest]. I've been experimenting with [Tactic] lately. How do you find it compared to other channels?
>
> Cheers,
> [Your Name]*

This template uses a shared interest to open naturally. It reads like a peer-to-peer conversation, not outreach. It invites a response without pressuring one, which tends to keep the exchange going.

### **Template 2: genuine compliment**

> *Hey [Name],
>
> Just read your article on [Specific Topic] - really cool take on [What]. I'm dabbling in something similar. Ever run into [Common Challenge]?
>
> Best,
> [Your Name]*‍

A genuine compliment on someone's work not only flatters but also shows that you've engaged with their content thoughtfully. This approach sets a positive tone and fosters mutual respect. It also naturally leads to a discussion about shared professional interests or challenges.

### **Template 3: mentioning a common connection**

> ‍*Hi [Name],
>
> [Common Connection's Name] said you're the go-to person for [Field]. I'm poking around in this field and could use a few pointers. Any quick tips/resources you can share?*
>
> ‍*Thanks,
> [Your Name]*

It’s one of the best-performing LinkedIn connection request templates out there. Mentioning a mutual connection provides a sense of trust and commonality. It leverages existing networks and suggests a pre-vetted credibility.

This approach often makes the recipient more open to engaging, as it feels like a recommendation rather than a cold call.

Inviting someone to a webinar or seminar that aligns with their interests is a tactful way to provide value upfront. It shows you’re not just looking to expand your network, but also to engage in meaningful knowledge exchange, making the connection more appealing and beneficial.

### **Template 11: seeking input for a specialized publication**

> [Your Name]*

It flatters the recipient by valuing their expertise and offers them a platform to share their insights. I found this approach is often met with enthusiasm, as it recognizes their professional reputation and offers them visibility.

## **6 LinkedIn connection message mistakes that kill your acceptance rate**

I’ve made every mistake out there, including sending bad LinkedIn connection messages, and let me tell you: I’ve probably lost more deals than you can count.

So, based on my experience I can confirm that it would be very wise to avoid the following six mistakes.

### 1. Sending low-effort, generic messages

Generic LinkedIn connection request with no personalization

* The message lacks substance, offering no engaging content or call to action
* There’s no mention of specific content I've created, which casts doubt on the sincerity of their interest (plus I immediately think the message is automated).
* The vague notion of *'growing together'* feels like a filler, not an authentic invitation to engage.

LinkedIn connection request offering vague help, no introduction

* The mention of *"similar connections"* is a weak attempt at finding common ground.
* Lacks any introduction that would place us in the same professional sphere.
* The offer to help is non-specific and comes off as insincere, failing to establish a genuine rapport.

Long LinkedIn connection request opening with a sales pitch

* This message jumps straight into a sales pitch, skipping the crucial step of gauging my interest or need.
* Overloaded with details, the lengthy message loses my attention before building a connection.

In summary, these types of connection messages miss the mark because they're crafted with minimal effort, lack personalization, and fail to provide a clear and engaging call to action.

### 2. Ignoring your acceptance rate

For years the Social Selling Index was the number people optimized here. LinkedIn has since walked away from it. Its own Sales Navigator resources now say a high SSI "doesn't always represent the efficacy of a sales person or correlate with measurable sales outcomes," and that the time and effort spent chasing one distracts from closing deals. The score page still exists. LinkedIn has simply stopped recommending you aim at it.

That leaves the number that does carry consequences: your acceptance rate.

LinkedIn doesn't publish how it uses that number, but every outreach tool that reports on it sees the same pattern: accounts with healthy acceptance keep their sending capacity, and accounts whose acceptance slides get less room to send.

### 3. Getting account penalties

People can report a connection request as spam, and LinkedIn keeps count. Enough reports and you get a warning, then a temporary restriction, and in the worst case you lose the account. The first two are recoverable, but slowly, and while you're restricted your outreach is off entirely.

Reports usually come from people who had no reason to hear from you in the first place. That makes it a targeting problem more than a volume one.

### 4. Sending more than your account has earned

LinkedIn doesn't publish its invitation limits, and the reason is that they aren't fixed — they move with your account. The safe working average is [around 100 connection requests a week](https://help.heyreach.io/en/articles/15128619-how-do-connection-request-limits-work-in-heyreach), which is roughly 20 a day across a normal working week.

A new or dormant account needs to stay under that: 10–15 requests a day for the first two weeks, then build it up gradually. An established account with a large network and a consistently high acceptance rate can push higher, up to around 140 a week.

### 5. Cutting the wrong words

Here's what often happens: you write the note you wanted to write, it runs over the character limit, and you start shaving words. Somehow, the filler survives and the specifics go. "I'd love to connect and explore synergies" stays. "Saw your post on pricing experiments" gets cut.

Fillers and generic phrases are the first to go, and there's usually more of them than you think.

If your message is still too long, you're probably trying to say two things when there's room for one. Drop the second and use that point in a follow-up.

### 6. Ignoring pending connections

Regular review and withdrawal of unaccepted requests help maintain a targeted outreach strategy and are increasingly important with the new LinkedIn limits.

I've found that waiting 1-2 weeks to see if a connection request is accepted is optimal. After this period, it's usually clear if the person is interested or active. Even though LinkedIn automatically clears pending invitations after 6 months, it's still a good idea to keep this clean if you're actively reaching out to people.

**How to remove a pending connection request:**

* **Access Invitations:** Go to your LinkedIn homepage and click on the 'My Network' icon at the top.
* **Navigate to Sent Invitations:** In the 'My Network' section, find and click on 'Manage' located near the top of the 'Invitations' section. This will take you to a page where you can see all the invitations you've sent and received.

LinkedIn invitation manager, Sent tab, with Withdraw button

* **Withdraw Sent Invitations:** Switch to the 'Sent' tab to view all the invitations you've sent. Here, you'll see a list of people to whom you've sent connection requests that haven't been accepted yet. Click 'Withdraw' next to the ones you want to pull back.
* **Confirm Withdrawal:** After clicking 'Withdraw', you may be asked to confirm your decision. Confirm it, and the invitation will be withdrawn.

Keep in mind the little-known LinkedIn rule that prevents you from resending it to the same person for 3 weeks. This forces a more strategic approach to whom we send requests.
